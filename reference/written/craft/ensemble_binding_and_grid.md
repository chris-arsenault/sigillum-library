# Ensemble Binding and the Grid — Corrections Encoded (Basin Aria Study)

What a deaf composer must hold in mind because it cannot be heard and
checked. Distilled from the Basin Aria correction cycle (2026-08/09): every
section exists because a decision read as defensible in the symbolic
projections and failed on listening. Cited as `ensemble_binding_and_grid:sN`.
Two kinds of content live here, and section 10 defines the difference:
**boundaries** (user-stated invariants - violating one is an error) and
**gradients** (corrections to a measured bias - directional, with the
uncorrected default still sometimes right). Only an explicit instruction or
current repair contract makes a boundary. Sections 1-5 also contain working
heuristics inferred from this piece; their imperative wording does not make
them universal musical laws. Never universalize a gradient.

For example, a composed relay can change timbre when contour, timing and
phrase continuation make the transfer audible. Independent counterpoint,
syncopation and an exposed solo entrance do not need to masquerade as answers.
The failure is an uncomposed relationship defended by a label. Judge the
relationship in the complete passage and preserve the user's actual invariants.

## 1. The grid layer is singular and its register roles are identity

One layer owns the pulse (Basin Aria: the handpan lattice). That layer's
identity is BOTH its rhythm and its register roles — which strokes are the
low kick-hand and which are the high color-hand. Re-pitching its low anchors
into the upper field preserves every attack and still destroys the beat,
because low-register strokes carry the pulse perceptually and high strokes
ring and smear. Harmonic re-coloring of a grid layer therefore:

- keeps low-role strokes low (nearest consonant low tone, same slot),
- declares the chord ROOT on the macro-beats (beat 1 / mid-bar), so the
  layer states the harmony rather than visiting its notes,
- never changes the onset lattice.

Corollary — the subdivision alignment law: other parts' short-value figures
either DOUBLE the grid's subdivision cells slot-for-slot or sit entirely in
grid-free slots (straddle). A figure that catches half of a grid cell and
hangs its other note in a silent slot is a phase error, not syncopation
(exception: runs of four or more, which establish their own stream).

## 2. The answer lane law: an interlocutor has one identity

Call-and-answer binds into a perceived conversation only when the answering
role is ONE voice: one timbre, one register lane, adjacent to or overlapping
the band of the line it answers. The literature's echo/answer devices
(Schubert's piano replies, comping under a held horn note) all satisfy this.

The failure mode found in Basin Aria: the "answer" rotated per bar across
harp-at-D4, clarinet-at-D2, viola-at-A3 — three octaves, four timbres. No
listener binds those into an interlocutor; they are unrelated events that
happen after the voice, and no performer can shape a line that has no lane.

Rules:
- Assign the answering role to one part and one lane per span; write it in
  the span's process text.
- An answer enters within a breath of the call's tail, overlapping it, and
  within roughly an octave of the call's band — or it carries the call's own
  contour so identity substitutes for proximity.
- Other parts may sound during answers, but only in established continuous
  roles (floor, grid, held field), never as competing responders.

## 3. Off-beat entries need a metric alibi

An entry off the macro-beats must be one of: (a) a pickup into the next
strong beat, (b) an overlap-answer under a held note in the answer lane,
(c) a written syncopation the grid supports. "Breath cushions" — bass notes
arriving on weak eighths at phrase ends — are a device from unmetered music
(recitative punctuation); over a running grid they read as a late player.
In groove textures the bass function locks to the macro-beats and fuses
with the grid layer's low anchors. Resonance-halo entries (Messiaen's
resonance chords, gamelan bloom) are legal only immediately after the
strike, ABOVE it, and quieter — a halo 1.5 beats late at equal level in a
lower register is a second event, not a halo.

## 4. Harmony is declared, not visited

A chord exists when its root is a primary driver on strong beats in the
bass function and/or the grid layer — not when its tones merely appear. A
key change exists only when the NEW key's distinguishing tones (the ones
absent from the old key) become structural and the new tonic is re-anchored
on strong beats, with the signature declared in the source (`key_change`).

The failure mode: "C major" bars against D natural minor that used no B
natural — pandiatonic root-emphasis wearing the home scale, drawing zero
accidentals, a key change in name only. Before claiming any modulation,
list the tones that differ between the keys and verify they are load-bearing
in the new bars. (D minor vs C major differ by ONE tone; D minor vs C minor
by two; vs Eb minor by three — the fewer the distinguishing tones, the more
deliberately they must be spent.)

## 5. A named device imports its preconditions

Using a technique's name (halo, echo, cushion, pedal, ostinato) does not
sanctify the notes. Before deploying one, state its precondition from the
literature and verify it holds here: halo = above the strike, immediate,
quieter; echo-answer = one lane, overlapping the tail; phrase-end pedal =
unmetered or non-groove context; ostinato = interior variation with a
directional vector. If the precondition fails, the device is not present —
only its label is.

## 6. The deaf composer's checklist

Run before calling a texture done. The perceptual projections (virtual
render: `spectrum_grid`, `masking_report`, `roughness_profile`,
`beat_salience`, `binding_check`, `ringing_grid`) are experimental work, not
part of the published inspection surface. When available, they can inform these;
the questions remain the review lens the views serve:

- Which parts establish the meter, and where does the passage deliberately
  suspend it? Do not fill an intended silence to satisfy the grid.
- For every entry: what event is it answering, and is it in that event's
  lane? If it is not an answer, what independent line, pickup, interruption
  or continuing figure makes its entrance intelligible?
- What is still RINGING (lv, long decay) at each entrance — not what is
  notated as sustaining? Would the entrance be audible through it?
- If the grid layer were muted, would the remaining parts still imply the
  meter? If the bass were muted, would the harmony still be declared?
- Which tones distinguish this bar's declared harmony from its neighbors,
  and does any voice actually spend them?

## 7. The phrase-break interlude (a tool, not a schedule)

A device I underuse: after a complete paragraph, break with one or two
bars of genuinely different material - new texture, new register pairing,
new rhythmic species - then resume. The reference (hand-composed Basin
Aria m15-16, after the 8-bar paragraph m7-14): voice and viola in bare
octaves on a three-note line with a bold leap (Bb-E-D), over a
bass-clarinet quarter-note hemiola into an f arrival. The interlude is a
structural comma; it resets the ear so the next phrase lands as an event.
It is one option among several at a paragraph seam (elision, direct
continuation, silence, a pickup handoff all remain legal); an interlude at
every seam would be its own monotony. Reach for it when consecutive
paragraphs share texture and the form needs air - and a species planted in
one (the hemiola) can be spent later as a motif.

## 8. The rhythm spectrum (a bias correction, not a quota)

My measured bias: lead phrases that chain one or two adjacent duration
values (.5s and 1s), which reads as recitation. The hand paragraph (m7-14)
shows the corrected center of gravity: .25 through 3 within eight bars,
rests inside the line, staccato-tipped gestures, one bar opening on a
repeated note. The correction is directional - when a lead is drafted,
check whether its durations cluster in a narrow band and whether that
narrowness is DOING something (a deliberate incantation, an even chant);
if it is just my default, widen it. A phrase of uniform values chosen on
purpose remains legal; uniformity as a habit is the defect.

## 9. Intervals are the phrase's physiognomy (a palette, not a checklist)

My measured bias: melodies that walk up the scale and walk back down. The
hand paragraph's vocabulary shows what a physiognomy looks like: a rising
third chain tipped staccato, a falling fourth mid-gesture, a sixteenth
turn into the second half, an octave-displaced crest approach, an
interlude launched by an augmented fourth. The correction: when a drafted
clause is purely scalar, ask whether the scalar shape is the point (a
composed run, a wedge line); if not, it is the default talking - reshape
with the palette. Purely scalar clauses exist in good music; consecutive
clauses that all share one arch because I wasn't choosing is the defect.
Inserting a mandatory leap per clause would be the same stamping disease
with a new stamp.

## 10. Gradients versus boundaries - how to encode feedback

Corrections arrive in two kinds and must be encoded as what they are.
**Boundaries** are user-stated invariants: the grid layer's rhythm is
untouchable, sub-C3 harp tones pair only as fifths/octaves. Violating one
is an error. **Gradients** are corrections to a measured bias: "use
interludes more," "less phrase-level monotony," "better intervals." These
name a direction and a reference example, not a rule; mechanically
universalizing a gradient ("always break phrases," "every clause needs a
leap") recreates the stamping the correction was against. When encoding
feedback here, state which kind it is, what bias it corrects, and when the
uncorrected default is still the right choice.

## 11. How this file grows

This document is the encoding target for listening feedback: when a
correction names a scheme (not a bar), restate it here at mechanism
altitude — which relationship failed and what musical change addresses it —
in the composer's own words, before or alongside repairing the piece. The
projections verify what is countable; this file holds what is audible.

## 12. Revision evidence must survive the export

A systemic revision traverses every affected passage with its whole ensemble.
Preserving an approved opening does not freeze provisional continuations or
later lead skeletons. Retain recognizable heads and directional events, then
develop their consequences. A small edit count, a larger rhythm-class count,
or fewer warning flags cannot establish musical improvement.

After drafting, identify the dimension that held attention and reread the others
separately. A line-led revision can introduce a new accompaniment habit: a held
macrobeat followed by the same three eighths, repeated across changing chords.
Compose how that supporting sentence develops; do not replace it with a quota
of rests, ornaments or different durations. Record what the second reading changed.

Validate analysis assumptions against the exported score. A virtual render that
applies a phrase's dynamic only to its first note, or keeps adding a completed
hairpin after later dynamics, can report a masking problem the export does not
contain. Check the notated directions and MIDI events before changing the music
to satisfy that model. Describe model limits explicitly. Preserve dissonances
whose preparation, resolution or formal job is clear; silence is not an error
because a binding scanner cannot find a simultaneous call.

Completion records describe the current source and export. If a pass is reverted,
mark its verdict as historical; never reuse its completed status as evidence for
the restored score. A score-and-MIDI reading is useful evidence, but it is not
a claim of hearing the intended instruments or of review by another musician.
